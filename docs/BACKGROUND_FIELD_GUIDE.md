# Background Field Guide

A practical guide for choosing atmosphere in Drift without letting atmosphere eat the deck.

## The fast decision

Ask four questions in order:

1. **Does the deck already contain a lot of visual information?**
   - Yes: start with Solid field, Printed matter, or Transparent.
   - No: Gradient weather, Luminous aura, and Darkroom void can carry more world-building.
2. **Should the room feel physical or optical?**
   - Physical: Printed matter, Solid field.
   - Optical: Gradient weather, Luminous aura, Darkroom void.
3. **Should the audience feel calm, wonder, or pressure?**
   - Calm: Quiet lane, Whisper presence, Quiet frame treatment.
   - Wonder: Light or Elemental lane, Balanced presence, Cinema print.
   - Pressure: Night or Tension lane, Balanced presence; reserve Statement for sparse slides.
4. **Is the background part of the deliverable?**
   - Yes: use an opaque family.
   - No, or compositing later: use Transparent.

## One rule above all

The background should be legible as a feeling and illegible as a trick.

A viewer may notice warmth, unease, age, rain, paper, projection, ocean depth, or fluorescence. They should not spend the first three seconds admiring procedural noise while ignoring the deck.

## Family guide

### Solid field

**Use when:**

- the slides are dense;
- typography must remain sovereign;
- you need a colour room rather than a visible effect;
- the carousel itself already supplies enough motion;
- the work is editorial, documentary, minimal, archival, or formally restrained.

**Best compositions:**

- **Pure field** for almost invisible atmosphere;
- **Projector wash** for low-key cinema and archive work;
- **Edge light** for thriller, noir, and graphic title slides;
- **Duotone floor** for travel, comedy, and clean horizon logic;
- **Paper tooth** when a flat colour feels too sterile;
- **Low halo** when the deck needs a quiet focal lift.

**Common failure:** raising intensity because the result appears “too simple.” Simplicity is the point. A solid field earns its place by staying out of the way.

### Gradient weather

**Use when:**

- the piece needs time of day or climate;
- lateral travel and horizon motion matter;
- the stage needs broad directional energy;
- the slides have enough negative space to tolerate a visible field.

**Best compositions:**

- **Legacy horizon** for broad, cinematic reliability;
- **Horizon melt** for memory, romance, and dream;
- **Diagonal weather** for drama and gathering pressure;
- **Radial dusk** for human warmth and intimate focus;
- **Prism bands** for music and pop without default neon mush;
- **Twin suns** for science fiction and unfamiliar optimism;
- **Split signal** for thriller and fracture;
- **Road mirage** for travel, heat, movement, and distance.

**Common failure:** pairing a high-contrast gradient with slides that already move through strong dark-light changes. Use Whisper presence or Quiet frame treatment before abandoning the family.

### Luminous aura

**Use when:**

- the work needs breath, tenderness, wonder, spirituality, or liquid light;
- the background should feel optical rather than environmental;
- the slides are clean enough to sit inside soft colour fields;
- you want visible animation without a literal place.

**Best compositions:**

- **Orbiting bloom** for an adaptable cinematic default;
- **Projector halo** for film, archive, and title cards;
- **Aurora veil** for wonder, weather, and quiet scale;
- **Stained light** for fantasy and ritual without literal iconography;
- **Liquid caustic** for ocean, glass, and speculative worlds;
- **Rose chamber** for romance and almost-touching tension;
- **Ice bloom** for alpine, crystalline, and clean futures;
- **Mandorla** for ritual, symmetry, and controlled sacred geometry.

**Common failure:** treating “aura” as permission to turn everything into a glowing blob. Change composition before pushing intensity. The field needs structure, not merely bloom.

### Printed matter

**Use when:**

- the deck should feel authored, handled, copied, printed, archived, or reported;
- digital perfection is weakening the material;
- the slides need texture that remains visually quiet;
- documentary, editorial, journalism, craft, memory, or comedy is central.

**Best compositions:**

- **Long fibres** for subtle stock and warm editorial work;
- **Contact sheet** for documentary evidence and photographic process;
- **Risograph cloud** for comedy, pop, and handmade colour;
- **Linen drift** for craft and tactile restraint;
- **Newsprint** for journalism and compressed graphic authority;
- **Silver emulsion** for archive, photography, and darkroom atmosphere;
- **Halftone field** for visible print logic and sparse graphic slides;
- **Dust archive** for memory, history, and damaged material.

**Common failure:** using maximum grain everywhere. Grain is not a proof of authenticity. Start with the authored study. Raise it only when the slide itself can survive the additional information.

### Darkroom void

**Use when:**

- darkness is active, not merely empty;
- the piece needs dread, scale, night, mystery, ritual, deep water, or damaged emulsion;
- sparse highlights can carry the atmosphere;
- the deck has strong typography and controlled slide brightness.

**Best compositions:**

- **Breathing slit** for horror and compressed red-black tension;
- **Eclipse** for science fiction, cosmic scale, and withheld information;
- **Ember smoke** for ritual, danger, and low heat;
- **Abyssal rays** for ocean depth and light entering from elsewhere;
- **Mineral fog** for mystery and geological pressure;
- **Rain negative** for noir, thriller, transit, and exposed film;
- **Chemical burn** for experimental work and unstable material;
- **Black tide** for dread, submerged motion, and slow threat.

**Common failure:** using Statement presence because the field looks dramatic in isolation. Check it with the brightest and darkest slides in the actual deck. Darkroom backgrounds can crush black slides and contaminate pale ones if not reviewed as a sequence.

## Treatment guide

### Quiet frame

Use when the slide contains text, faces, diagrams, or several bright regions. It reduces local contrast around the focal centre without flattening the outer world.

Do not read “quiet” as “weak.” It is the most editorial treatment and often the right final answer.

### Cinema print

Use when the palette needs a little more density and highlight warmth. It should feel like a restrained print response, not an Instagram filter.

It works especially well with tungsten, desert, rose, ochre, scarlet, and daylight palettes.

### Graphic cut

Use when shape separation matters: music, pop, title cards, collage, bold typography, science fiction, or a deck with large flat-colour slides.

It is deliberately bounded. If you need aggressive posterisation, change the slide art rather than asking the background to impersonate it.

### Weathered stock

Use when the field should feel faded, copied, exposed, old, mineral, dusty, or materially imperfect. Blacks lift slightly and saturation relaxes.

It works well with archive, memory, journalism, road, darkroom, and experimental work. It is usually wrong for clinical, glossy, or highly controlled futurism unless used as friction.

## Presence guide

### Whisper

Use by default when:

- the deck is text-heavy;
- the slide backgrounds vary wildly;
- the carousel path is already expressive;
- the video includes a pinned presenter;
- the final piece will be watched on a phone.

### Balanced

Use when the background is part of the storytelling but not a title event. This is the safest starting point for most authored studies.

### Statement

Use selectively:

- opening or closing title;
- sparse quote slides;
- chapter breaks;
- one-image slides;
- moments where the background temporarily becomes a scene partner.

Do not assume the entire carousel needs one presence profile. Export separate sections when the narrative benefits from a stronger chapter break.

## Reviewing against a real deck

Never approve a background from the demo slides alone.

Review at least:

1. the brightest slide;
2. the darkest slide;
3. the slide with the most text;
4. the slide with a face near an edge;
5. the slide with the weakest contrast;
6. the title slide;
7. a rapid transition between two very different slides;
8. the pinned presenter, when enabled.

Then review the sequence at target phone size. A field that looks elegant on a desktop may become noisy once every slide occupies a small portion of a 9:16 screen.

## Aspect-ratio review

### 9:16

- Watch for broad horizontal fields becoming visually empty.
- Prefer vertical weather, projector halos, slits, fibres, rain, and stacked horizons.
- Keep Statement presence rare; the stage is already narrow and visually concentrated.

### 4:5

- Most compositions remain balanced.
- Check cards near the vertical edges against edge lights and void rays.
- Good default for showing a deck with enough room for atmosphere.

### 1:1

- Radial and symmetrical fields become stronger.
- Mandorla, eclipse, halo, dusk, and bloom can become too literal; lower intensity before abandoning them.

### 16:9

- Horizons, weather fronts, road mirage, prism bands, and wide projector wash gain authority.
- Vertical slits and narrow halos may need a different recut.
- Wide empty areas make paper texture more visible; inspect grain at full resolution.

## Transparent output

Transparent means no background shader is rendered.

Use it when:

- compositing over footage later;
- creating reusable slide motion elements;
- delivering PNG stills or PNG sequences with alpha;
- the destination already supplies its own atmosphere.

H.264 does not preserve alpha in this export path. A “transparent MP4” is not promised because it would be false.

## When to stop adjusting

Stop when:

- the deck reads immediately;
- the atmosphere feels coherent across all slides;
- no single background event steals the eye from a slide;
- the first and last frame close without a visible jump;
- the phone-size version still feels intentional;
- the field remains recognisable after lowering intensity by ten percent.

The last test matters. If the background falls apart when slightly quieter, it was probably relying on spectacle rather than structure.
